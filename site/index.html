What is CVD?

CVD means Chemical Vapour Deposition. It forms a thin solid film on a wafer through chemical reactions involving gases or vapours. The film stays on the wafer; volatile reaction products and unused gases leave through the exhaust. These films can insulate, connect or protect chip features, or form semiconductor layers.

How CVD works · gas roles · CVD types

How the film forms

  1. Prepare the surface. Load the prepared wafer and establish the recipe’s temperature and pressure.
  2. Deliver the chemicals. Feed the precursor and required co-reactants. Liquid precursors, such as TEOS, are vaporised first.
  3. Activate the reaction. Heat drives thermal CVD. In PECVD, RF plasma assists the chemistry, usually with wafer heating too.
  4. Grow the solid film. Species react at the wafer surface. Atoms become part of the growing layer.
  5. Remove reaction products. Exhaust carries away volatile by-products and unused gases during growth. Finish by stopping reactants, purging and cooling.

What each term means

Wafer
The silicon base used to build the chip.
Precursor
A starting compound that supplies atoms for the film.
Co-reactant
Another chemical that reacts with the precursor to form the desired deposit.
Carrier / diluent
Moves or dilutes the feed. Examples here are argon (Ar) and helium (He).
Plasma
Plasma is an ionised gas, often called the fourth state of matter. It forms when energy frees electrons from gas atoms or molecules. In PECVD, it contains free electrons, ions and neutral particles.
RF power
RF means radio frequency. The RF generator supplies electrical power that creates and sustains the plasma in these PECVD presets.
Deposit / film
The solid layer that remains. Its material determines its function.
By-product
Other reaction products. Volatile products leave through the exhaust.

Polysilicon and epitaxial silicon

Polysilicon

Polysilicon, or polycrystalline silicon, is silicon made up of many small crystal grains separated by grain boundaries. The grains can have different crystal orientations.

The silane LPCVD preset deposits a polysilicon film on the heated wafer. With suitable doping and patterning, polysilicon can form gate electrodes and resistors.

Epitaxial silicon

Epitaxial silicon is a single-crystal silicon layer grown on a crystalline silicon wafer. Its atomic lattice follows the underlying wafer’s crystal orientation.

The SiCl₄/H₂ APCVD preset grows this layer on a prepared crystalline silicon surface. It provides a crystalline layer for building semiconductor devices.

Purity is a separate property. Polysilicon's name describes its crystal-grain structure. High-purity bulk polysilicon can be produced by purifying metallurgical-grade silicon for wafer manufacture. This simulator shows a thin polysilicon film deposited from silane.

The material grade and manufacturing process determine chemical purity. Crystal structures · Polysilicon films · Epitaxial growth · Bulk silicon and wafer manufacture

SiH4 (gas)→Si (solid)+2H2 (gas)

Silicon stays in the polysilicon film; hydrogen leaves as gas. This recipe uses thermal activation. Different co-reactants can produce a different film from the same precursor.

What is PECVD?

Plasma-enhanced chemical vapour deposition (PECVD) uses plasma to make precursor gases and other reactants more reactive. Energetic electrons help break gas molecules into reactive particles. These particles react at the wafer surface to form a thin solid film. Plasma activation often allows lower wafer temperatures than thermal CVD.

A common RF frequency is 13.56 MHz. Equipment can use other frequencies or combinations. The wafer temperature depends on the recipe. In this lab, the silane oxide PECVD example uses 300 °C, and the TEOS oxide example starts at 350 °C within its 350–400 °C reference window.
